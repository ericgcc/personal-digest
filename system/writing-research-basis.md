# Writing Reference—Research Basis
This file records the expert sources behind the Digest System writing references and how their ideas are used. It is **provenance, not a runtime checklist**. Operational guidance belongs in the other writing-reference files.

Folder placement in the research library is intentionally ignored here. Sources are classified by what they contribute.

## Core analytical sources
### David Rosenwasser & Jill Stephen—*Writing Analytically*
Primary contribution: the intellectual mechanics behind analytical and synthesized writing.

Adopted ideas:

* analysis as inquiry into meaning rather than passive summary or immediate judgment;
* evidence used to test and develop ideas, not merely decorate a predetermined thesis;
* significant details, patterns, anomalies, implicit assumptions, implications, and repeated reformulation as idea-generating moves;
* tolerance for uncertainty and competing explanations;
* the idea that strong insights can be modest in scope;
* careful distinction between summary and the interpretive leap of analysis.

Adaptation for Digest: keep the analytical moves; discard academic-paper form as a voice model.

### Wayne Booth, Gregory Colomb, Joseph Williams et al.—*The Craft of Research*
Primary contribution: disciplined claims and source relationships.

Adopted ideas:

* claims supported by reasons and evidence;
* warrants and assumptions examined when they matter;
* meaningful objections and alternatives acknowledged rather than ritual "balance";
* note **why** sources agree or disagree, not merely that they do;
* source annotation as active response rather than passive accumulation.

Adaptation for Digest: use the argument discipline internally while keeping visible prose editorial rather than scholarly.

### Gerald Graff & Cathy Birkenstein—*They Say / I Say*
Primary contribution: sources as positions in an ongoing conversation.

Adopted ideas:

* identify what a source is responding to;
* use engagement with other positions to generate ideas;
* map claims relative to other claims when comparison is editorially useful;
* conversational and public-facing prose can carry serious analysis.

Adaptation for Digest: use the rhetorical moves, **not** the literal sentence templates or academic attribution formulas.

## Core prose and clarity sources
### Joseph M. Williams & Joseph Bizup—*Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*
Primary contribution: sentence clarity, cohesion, emphasis, and reader processing.

Adopted ideas:

* important characters/concepts make strong grammatical subjects;
* important actions belong in verbs;
* manage old-to-new information flow;
* use sentence endings for emphasis;
* treat style as a set of reasoned choices rather than folklore.

### Steven Pinker—*The Sense of Style*
Primary contribution: reader-centered nonfiction and the cognitive reasons behind clarity.

Adopted ideas:

* guard against the curse of knowledge;
* clarity and coherence can coexist with flair;
* prefer principles with reasons over mechanical rules;
* write as though showing the reader something, not documenting the act of writing.

### William Zinsser—*On Writing Well*
Primary contribution: simplicity, economy, voice, rewriting, and readable nonfiction.

Adopted ideas:

* remove clutter without draining personality;
* rewrite for precision and strength;
* protect unity and the reader's path;
* read prose aloud to hear problems invisible on the page;
* say something people actually want to read.

### Verlyn Klinkenborg—*Several Short Sentences About Writing*
Primary contribution: sentence-level control and rhythm.

Adopted ideas:

* sentence length does not measure sophistication;
* short sentences expose ambiguity and weak construction;
* strong long sentences can be built from clear smaller relationships;
* remove needless preambles and postludes;
* rhythm comes from deliberate placement and variation;
* "flow" is the reader's experience, not proof that the first fluent draft is good.

Adaptation for Digest: do not imitate the book's fragment-heavy surface style; use its compositional discipline.

## Core editorial and nonfiction sources
### Donald Murray—*Writing for Your Readers*
Primary contribution: reader questions, leads, endings, specificity, and invisible editorial craft.

Adopted ideas:

* anticipate the questions a reader will naturally ask;
* order information so answers arrive when needed;
* leads should point honestly into the piece;
* endings deserve emphasis but should stop once the final strong information is delivered;
* use specifics and active verbs; read aloud.

### Francis Flaherty—*The Elements of Story*
Primary contribution: curiosity and narrative movement in nonfiction.

Adopted ideas:

* a good lead creates a useful question the body can answer;
* contrast and unexpected juxtaposition can generate reader interest;
* movement and necessary background can be interwoven rather than delivered in separate blocks.

Adaptation for Digest: use narrative logic without manufacturing suspense or scenes.

### Alice LaPlante—*The Making of a Story*
Primary contribution: concrete detail, discovery, and the balance between showing and telling.

Adopted ideas:

* "show, don't tell" is not a universal rule;
* exact concrete details can carry understanding better than symbolic or abstract overstatement;
* avoid a self-consciously "writerly" voice;
* meaning should emerge from the particulars rather than be imposed through heavy symbolism.

Adaptation for Digest: source-grounded examples and details only; never fictionalize.

### Lee Gutkind—*The Art of Creative Nonfiction*
Primary contribution: factual integrity combined with literary craft.

Adopted ideas:

* focus determines what to omit;
* specificity, framing, and narrative devices can make factual material compelling;
* nonfiction can be vivid without surrendering accuracy.

Adaptation for Digest: no invented scenes, dialogue, interiority, or personal experience.

### Barry Tarshis—*How to Write Like a Pro*
Primary contribution: reader-sensitive organization and a governing thought.

Adopted ideas:

* the reader has only the words on the page to reconstruct what the writer already understands;
* paragraphs need a central controlling idea rather than a pile of related information.

### Jan Yager—*Effective Business & Nonfiction Writing*
Primary contribution: clear, thoughtful prose, audience awareness, organization, and rewriting.

Role in system: supporting reference. Its strongest principles reinforce more detailed guidance from Williams, Pinker, Zinsser, and Murray rather than adding a separate method.

## Sources used cautiously or not made normative
### *WriteType: Personality Types and Writing Styles*
The book offers useful reminders that writers and audiences differ and that analytical writers can neglect reader sensitivity or detail. Its personality-type framework is not strong enough to govern the Digest System's editorial decisions, so it is **not a core normative source**. We retain only the general reminder to adapt voice and reader orientation to the task.

## AI-pattern and naturalness sources
### Wikipedia—"Signs of AI writing"
Used as a broad field guide to recurring patterns, with its own caveat preserved: the list is descriptive rather than prescriptive, individual signs are not proof, and fixing surface tells without fixing underlying problems is the wrong goal.

Most useful observations for this system:

* statistical smoothing from specific facts toward generic importance;
* superficial significance language;
* vague source attribution and false consensus;
* avoidance of plain `is/are/has` constructions in favor of inflated verbs;
* vague relationship language;
* repeated negative parallelism, triplets, and other formulaic rhetorical shapes;
* overuse—not mere use—of em dashes and similar devices;
* formulaic challenge/future-outlook conclusions;
* the value of being able to explain editorial choices.

### Charlie Fink—"The Seven Deadly Tells of AI Writing" (*Forbes*)
Used as a practitioner heuristic, not empirical law. Useful warnings include repeated contrastive framing, rhetorical Q&A, triplet framing, unnecessary dashes, inspirational pivots toward fake profundity, unattributed universal authority, and fabricated quotations.

### Bert Maxwell—"Top Signs Your Writing Was Generated by AI" (*Wandering Educators*)
Useful practitioner observations: transition crutches, metronomic rhythm, generic vocabulary, weak fact checking, structural symmetry, and missing signs of a particular mind.

Adaptation for Digest: recommendations to insert anecdotes, personal details, slang, or artificial irregularity are **not** adopted. The system's editorial voice must never fabricate a human biography.

### "How To Tell If Someone Is Secretly Writing With AI"—*The Daily Draft*
Most useful contribution: detector scores are unreliable and a stronger signal of genuine authorship is an explainable body of knowledge and editorial decisions—specific details, research paths, mistakes, changes of mind, and the ability to discuss the material beyond a polished artifact.

Adaptation for Digest: translate "personal footprint" into **editorial cognition**: explainable selection, omission, evidence, qualification, and source relationships.

### MonarchPanda—"The Only 5 Signs of AI Writing You Need to Remove in Your Text Now"
Useful heuristics: overly perfect structure, emotional flatness, jargon/transition addiction, generic examples, and disclaimer or hedging overload.

Adaptation for Digest: do not simulate emotion or lived experience. Repair genericity with source-grounded specificity and repair symmetry by letting the material determine form.

## Synthesis of the research
The sources converge on a useful division of labor:

1. **Reason from evidence before deciding the frame.**
2. **Select the few details that actually carry the idea.**
3. **Organize for the reader's evolving understanding.**
4. **Express actions and relationships concretely.**
5. **Let rhetorical shape follow the material rather than a template.**
6. **Revise for rhythm, economy, and proportion only after the thought is sound.**
7. **Treat naturalness as a consequence of specific, explainable editorial choices—not as cosmetic "humanization."**
