# Writing Reference—Style Application
The shared writing references provide techniques; **the selected style determines which techniques may control the composition**. This guide prevents a strong shared prose system from collapsing the canonical styles into one voice or one source relationship.

## Style map
| Dimension | Concise | Detailed | Curated Discovery | Synthesis MAX |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary editorial unit | One source | One source | One selected idea, normally source-centered | One concrete shared subject explained through at least two sources |
| Cross-source synthesis | Prohibited | Prohibited | Occasional; only when it materially improves one selected idea | Mandatory in every narrative thread when the grouping passes the synthesis tests |
| Analytical intensity | Light | Moderate within source | High for selection and source interpretation | Highest internally; reader-facing explanation remains concrete |
| Source-thesis preservation | Very high | Very high | High by default | Sources may be reframed, but each source's main explanatory value must remain recoverable |
| Editorial transformation | Minimal | Moderate restructuring for clarity | Strong reframing around the best idea | Integrated explanation first; supported cross-source inference second |
| Prose energy | Brisk | Patient | Curious, magazine-like | Explanatory and analytical |
| Typical reader experience | Scan | Understand each source | Discover a few ideas worth keeping | Understand material better through relationships no single source provides |

Shared quality does not imply shared behavior.

## Concise—compression without synthesis
The editor's question is:

**What is the smallest faithful statement of why this source deserves attention?**

Use the reasoning guide only to find the source's central value and protect against distortion. Use the prose guide aggressively for clarity and economy. Naturalness matters mainly at the corpus level: entries must not sound like repeated template fills.

Do not add an editorial inference simply because the entry feels too plain. Plain and useful is success.

## Detailed—explanation without cross-source interpretation
The editor's question is:

**What does this source actually argue, show, teach, or report, and what detail is necessary to understand it well?**

Analysis happens inside the source: identify hierarchy, mechanism, evidence, examples, and qualifications. Reorder material for reader comprehension, but do not derive a larger conclusion from neighboring sources.

The prose can be richer than Concise, but the editorial presence should remain quieter than Curated Discovery.

## Curated Discovery—selection and interpretation, source-first by default
The editor's question is:

**What is the strongest idea here that is worth keeping, and how can I tell that idea as a small, satisfying piece of nonfiction?**

This style uses substantial analytical skill, but the analysis normally serves **discovery and sharpening**, not theory-building across the corpus.

Start with the strongest thesis or reader promise of an individual source. Reframe it when that makes the idea clearer, more memorable, or more useful. Bring in another source only when it materially improves that same idea and its contribution is real.

Use cross-source synthesis sparingly. A heterogeneous edition is healthy. Diversity is not a framing failure.

The voice may show more curiosity, tonal movement, and editorial personality than Synthesis MAX. Concrete details, interesting examples, and small surprises are especially valuable when they make the selected idea vivid.

**Boundary:** Curated Discovery should leave the reader remembering several excellent ideas. It should not make unrelated ideas disappear into a master theory.

## Synthesis MAX—evidence-led integrative synthesis
The editor's question is:

**What concrete topic, question, mechanism, development, or tension do at least two sources help the reader understand better together—and what does each source add?**

The unit is not a source. It is a concrete shared subject explained through an integrated thread whose evidence is distributed across at least two substantively contributing sources. Cross-source synthesis is the defining composition rule; reader understanding is the governing quality criterion.

Do the deepest analytical work before writing. Search for reinforcement, qualification, contradiction, complementarity, common mechanism, or a productive anomaly. Then test the proposed synthesis against alternative explanations, source-specific caveats, comprehension, and semantic distance.

Prefer two or three sources when they are sufficient to explain the subject clearly. Use four or more only when every source contributes to the same concrete topic, question, mechanism, development, or tension without requiring a higher-level abstraction merely to hold the group together. Never create a single-source thread, and do not count an ornamental analogy or supporting citation as a substantive second source.

Orient the reader in the concrete shared subject before presenting a compressed interpretation. The source order should disappear into the logic of one integrated explanation, not become a sequence of summaries. The reader should be able to see what each source contributes, why each piece of evidence appears where it does, and which parts are the briefing's inference.

The voice should feel like a lucid analytical magazine briefing, not a literature review or conceptual performance: few explicit source-attribution formulas, no ritual survey of positions, no academic throat-clearing, and no grand conclusion merely because multiple sources were involved.

**Boundary:** Synthesis MAX earns its identity when at least two sources jointly make a concrete subject easier to understand than separate summaries would. Write so the reader understands the material through the synthesis, not so the reader admires the synthesis itself.

## Curated Discovery vs. Synthesis MAX
This distinction is non-negotiable.

### Curated Discovery
`source-worthy idea → editorial mini-essay → optional complementary source`

A source's own valuable thesis is normally the anchor. The editor selects, clarifies, reframes, and tells it well.

### Synthesis MAX
`concrete shared subject → integrated source contributions → clear explanation → tested synthesis or implication`

The cross-source relationship defines the composition, but it must be organized around something concrete the reader can understand. Individual source theses may be reframed as components of the integrated explanation, but their main explanatory value must remain recoverable. If combining sources makes it harder to understand what one of them teaches, reject or regroup the thread.

A simple boundary test:

* A Synthesis MAX thread must contain at least two sources that materially change or deepen the explanation. If removing the second source leaves essentially the same idea, the piece belongs naturally in **Curated Discovery**.
* The sources must share a concrete subject that can be stated plainly before the editorial conclusion. If they belong together only after translating them into a broad abstract principle, do not group them.
* Combining the sources must make the underlying material easier to understand, not merely yield a more abstract interpretation.

## Rhetorical differentiation
Do not use shared naturalness guidance to equalize tone.

* **Concise:** short, direct, minimally interpretive. Variation comes from the source, not flourish.
* **Detailed:** calm and explanatory. The pleasure comes from lucid understanding.
* **Curated Discovery:** lively and selective. It can linger on an apt detail, a surprising mechanism, or an interesting consequence.
* **Synthesis MAX:** explanatory, connective, and analytical. Its energy comes from understanding a concrete subject more clearly as the relationship becomes visible—not from maximizing abstraction.

The same sentence pattern, opening device, or level of abstraction should not become a house reflex across all four styles.
