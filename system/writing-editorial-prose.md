# Writing Reference—Editorial Prose
This guide governs how a sound editorial idea becomes **clear, engaging nonfiction**. It does not define what a style selects or whether sources are synthesized.

The target is prose that feels composed for a reader: precise without stiffness, lively without performance, and easy to follow without flattening the material.

## Write from the reader's side of the page
The writer already knows the material; the reader does not. Never make the reader reconstruct context that exists only in the editor's head.

Before a sentence depends on a term, actor, distinction, or causal step, ask whether the reader has enough information to understand it. Supply the minimum orientation required, then move.

Avoid the curse of knowledge:

* Prefer a concrete mechanism over an expert shorthand when both carry the same meaning.
* Introduce unfamiliar terminology before leaning on it.
* Do not show the research process simply to prove that research occurred.
* Assume intelligence, not prior exposure to every detail.

## Bridge domain knowledge without flattening it
The reader may be capable and well informed without knowing this source's field, institution, vocabulary, history, or conventions. Treat specialized knowledge as something the prose can make available, not as an entrance requirement.

When a domain term is necessary, a useful sequence is:

`plain-language meaning or function → necessary domain term → consequence or significance`

This is not a rigid sentence template. It is an information-ordering principle: give the reader enough meaning to understand the label, then show why the concept belongs in the piece.

* Retain a specialized term when it supplies necessary precision, is central to the idea, or will be reused. Explain it at the point of use through its function, effect, referent, consequence, or a concise example.
* If a term appears only once and an ordinary phrase carries the same meaning, prefer the ordinary phrase.
* Treat professional shorthand, acronyms, named frameworks, institutions, historical references, and field-specific metaphors as potentially unfamiliar—not only scientific or technical vocabulary.
* Introduce new concepts at a rate the explanation can support. Do not make the reader hold several undefined actors, labels, or distinctions in working memory while also interpreting the conclusion.
* Do not stack an unfamiliar term, a new actor or context, a metaphor, and an editorial inference in the same sentence. Separate orientation from interpretation.
* Explain without adopting a classroom voice. A short functional clause is often enough; use a fuller example only when it materially improves understanding.

The goal is not to remove the vocabulary that makes a domain precise. It is to make that vocabulary learnable within the reading experience.

## Present the thing, not the performance of knowing it
For general-interest editorial prose, aim for the stance of a capable editor showing an intelligent peer something worth seeing. The research and analysis may have been difficult; the prose should present their result rather than reenact their difficulty.

* Point the reader toward the fact, mechanism, contrast, or relationship itself.
* Keep the writer and the act of analysis in the background unless the selected style genuinely needs them.
* Do not parade source count, research effort, or methodological throat-clearing as a substitute for authority.
* Trust the reader to connect obvious dots; explain the non-obvious relationship.
* Treat the reader as an equal who lacks this particular context, not as a student waiting for a lecture.

This is compatible with analytical depth: Synthesis MAX can do difficult reasoning internally and still read like editorial nonfiction rather than like a paper reporting its method.

## Make the prose transparent to the thought
The reader should spend attention on the idea, not on decoding the sentence.

Prefer:

* important actors or concepts as grammatical subjects;
* important actions as verbs rather than buried nouns;
* ordinary exact verbs over inflated substitutes;
* simple `is`, `are`, and `has` constructions when they are the clearest choice;
* concrete relationship verbs such as `causes`, `uses`, `depends on`, `changes`, `limits`, or `contradicts` instead of vague association language.

Do not "improve" a plain sentence merely by making it sound more formal.

## Manage information flow
Good sentences tell the reader both **where they are** and **where to look next**.

* Put familiar or already established information early when it helps continuity.
* Place genuinely new or emphatic information where it receives stress, often near the end.
* Keep subjects stable long enough for a paragraph to cohere, then signal a real change of subject or abstraction level.
* Let adjacent sentences carry obvious relationships without unnecessary transition labels.

A paragraph should not be a bag of individually polished sentences. Its sentences should accumulate meaning.

## Build paragraphs around jobs
Each paragraph should perform a recognizable job in the developing idea: orient, establish, explain, exemplify, test, qualify, contrast, extend, or resolve.

Do not require every paragraph to do every job. Unevenness is often a sign that the material, rather than a template, determined the shape.

A useful internal question is: **What becomes possible for the reader after this paragraph that was not possible before it?**

If the answer is "nothing; it restates the point more elegantly," cut or merge it.

## Open with a real reason to continue
A lead should point into the piece, not decorate its entrance.

Depending on the material, begin with:

* a concrete fact that creates a useful question;
* an anomaly or contradiction;
* a consequence the reader can immediately grasp;
* a mechanism already in motion;
* a precise claim when no setup is needed;
* a brief source-grounded scene or example when it genuinely clarifies the idea.

Do not use a surprising opening that promises a different story from the one that follows. Do not delay the subject merely to create suspense.

## End when the thought is complete
The ending carries disproportionate emphasis. Use that emphasis for the strongest final piece of information, implication, qualification, or resolution the material earns.

Do not append a second explanation of what the reader should think or feel. Deliver the solid information and stop.

This is especially important in a digest: neat "lesson learned" bows often weaken a section that already made its point.

## Show and tell deliberately
"Show, don't tell" is not a universal rule.

* **Tell** when direct explanation is the fastest, clearest way to transfer understanding.
* **Show** with a concrete example, number, observed detail, sequence, or small scene when it makes an abstract mechanism visible.

Use the **right** concrete detail, not the maximum number of details. A vivid detail that does not advance the idea is still clutter.

Never invent scenes, sensory detail, quotations, experiences, or reactions to make digest prose feel human.

## Sentence rhythm is functional
Sentence length is not a proxy for intelligence. Short sentences can carry complex ideas; long sentences are useful when their syntax makes a real relationship easier to see.

* Vary length because the thought changes pace, not to hit a stylistic quota.
* Use a short sentence for emphasis only when the emphasis is earned.
* Use longer sentences when qualification, comparison, or causal structure benefits from being held together.
* Remove unnecessary preambles and postludes.
* Read important passages aloud internally; repeated cadence, accidental rhyme, or mechanical transitions become easier to hear.

"Flow" is an effect on the reader, not an excuse to accept the first fluent draft.

## Voice is a rhetorical position
Natural prose is not raw or accidental. The style establishes who is speaking to the reader and from what distance.

Maintain the selected style's declared character, but make the voice feel inhabited rather than templated:

* choose diction appropriate to the subject rather than a universal house vocabulary;
* allow plainness beside sophistication;
* let confidence come from specificity and reasoning rather than assertive tone;
* use humor, metaphor, colloquial phrasing, or elegance only when it clarifies or suits the style.

Do not inject fake personality. The digest's human quality should come from judgment, specificity, rhythm, and proportion.

## Titles and decks
A title should sharpen the idea. A deck should help the reader enter it.

* Prefer concrete tension, mechanism, consequence, or distinction over abstract slogans.
* Do not inflate a modest idea to make the title sound consequential.
* Avoid repeatedly using the same rhetorical construction across titles.
* A deck should add orientation or precision, not paraphrase the title.

## Revision pass
After the structural and clarity edits, read the prose once for the reader's experience:

* Can the reader picture or understand what the important nouns refer to?
* Could an intelligent reader outside this specific domain explain what happened or how it works and why it matters?
* Are necessary specialized terms understandable at the point where the prose begins to depend on them?
* Are the important actions actually expressed as actions?
* Does new information arrive in an order the reader can absorb?
* Has any sentence combined unfamiliar vocabulary, missing context, metaphor, and inference into one decoding task?
* Does each paragraph advance rather than orbit the idea?
* Are specifics doing explanatory work?
* Is the final sentence the right place to stop?
* Does the rhythm change with the material rather than repeat mechanically?

If the prose sounds polished but generic, return to the source and recover the detail or distinction that made the idea worth selecting.
