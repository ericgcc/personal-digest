# Detailed

A structured per-source digest designed to preserve the important context, nuance and useful detail of each source while remaining substantially faster to read than the original material.
## Summary mode
Keep every source independent.

For each reviewed source worth retaining, produce a structured summary that captures:

1. The central idea, development or argument.
2. The most important supporting details, evidence, examples or mechanisms.
3. Relevant qualifications, limitations, trade-offs or uncertainty.
4. The practical or conceptual significance when reasonably supported by the source.

Summarize the source as a coherent whole rather than extracting a disconnected list of facts.

Do not combine multiple sources into one argument or derive conclusions from their relationship. Source independence is part of this style.

Any examples in these instructions are illustrative only. Never treat them as an exhaustive list, preferred taxonomy, required output, or signal to search for those specific topics.

## Organization

Group source summaries under broad topical headings when this makes the digest easier to navigate.

Derive topic headings from the actual source set. Do not assume predefined categories or look for specific subject areas unless they are present in the material or requested by custom instructions.

Topic headings should be descriptive, broad enough to group genuinely related sources, and specific enough to improve navigation.

Topic grouping is organizational only. It must not imply that grouped sources agree, reinforce one another or form a shared narrative.

If the source set is small, heterogeneous or does not support useful grouping, present the summaries directly without forcing thematic categories.

Within every topic, keep each source clearly identifiable and independent.

## Length and density

Aim for approximately 45–75 seconds of reading per substantive source.

Typical summaries should be roughly 120–220 words, but let information density determine length.

Use less space for simple announcements, short essays or incremental updates.

Use more space for unusually rich technical explanations, analyses, tutorials, case studies or arguments when the extra detail materially improves understanding.

Never pad a source merely to reach a target length.

Prefer concise explanation over exhaustive coverage.

## Required structure

For each retained source include:

### Source or publication name

**Article or item title**

Begin with a short paragraph that explains the source’s central idea and enough context to understand why it matters.

Then surface the most useful details using two to five concise bullet points when appropriate.

Useful bullet content may include:

- Important evidence or supporting arguments.
- Technical mechanisms or implementation details.
- Concrete examples.
- Techniques, workflows or recommendations presented by the source.
- Trade-offs, limitations or caveats.
- Consequences or implications explicitly supported by the source.

These are illustrative categories only. Include whichever types of details are actually important in the source.

Use prose instead of bullets when the source depends on a sequential argument and breaking it apart would reduce clarity.

Do not mechanically fill every category. Include only details that materially improve understanding.

Link the title directly to the original source when a valid locator exists. If the source exists only in the email and no reliable locator is available, render the title as plain text and preserve email-only provenance instead of inventing a link.

## Multiple items within one source

When a newsletter or source contains several distinct items, do not automatically summarize all of them.

Prioritize the items with the highest information value.

If several items are independently substantial, they may receive separate subentries under the same source.

Avoid turning a newsletter containing many links into an exhaustive link roundup.

## Cross-source overlap

When several sources cover the same event, announcement or subject, keep their summaries independent.

Compress repeated background and emphasize what is distinctive about each source, such as additional evidence, a different interpretation, more technical detail, a stronger example, an important criticism or limitation, or a different practical consequence.

These possibilities are illustrative, not a required checklist.

Do not create a shared conclusion from overlapping sources.

If repetition adds no meaningful information, keep the summaries brief rather than rewarding duplication with extra space.
## Selection and filtering
Preserve reasonably broad source coverage, but still remove obvious noise.

Omit or strongly de-emphasize:
- Pure promotional or sponsored material.
- Administrative messages.
- Welcome emails.
- Exact duplicates.
- Empty link collections with insufficient accessible content.
- Material with too little substance to summarize reliably.

Do not omit a substantive source simply because it does not fit a dominant topic.

When no custom focus is provided, prioritize clarity, information value, explanatory depth, practical significance and novelty.

## Fidelity and nuance
Preserve the source’s actual meaning and level of certainty.

Clearly distinguish between:

- What the source reports or demonstrates.
- What the author argues or interprets.
- What the author recommends.
- What remains uncertain or speculative.

Do not transform prediction into fact.

Do not introduce recommendations, implications or conclusions that go materially beyond the source.

Preserve important caveats when omitting them would make the summary misleading.

If only partial content is available, summarize only what the available material supports.
## Ending rules

Finish after the final source summary.

Do not include:

- A Big Picture section.
- Cross-source trends or patterns.
- A concluding synthesis.
- Generic takeaways.
- A motivational closing.
- A separate source catalog unless explicitly requested.
## Quality control

Before returning the digest, verify that:

- Every retained source remains individually identifiable.
- Topic grouping improves navigation without becoming synthesis.
- Topic headings emerge from the source set rather than from predefined categories.
- Each summary communicates the central idea before secondary detail.
- Important evidence, mechanisms and examples are preserved when useful.
- Relevant caveats, uncertainty and trade-offs are not lost.
- Bullet points contain substantive information rather than fragmented prose.
- Repeated background across sources is compressed.
- No unsupported cross-source conclusions have been introduced.
- No source has been padded merely to meet a length target.
- Examples in these instructions have not been treated as required topics or output categories.
- Every available source link points to the correct original source, and email-only sources are not given fabricated links.
- The result preserves useful context and nuance while remaining a per-source digest rather than a cross-source editorial synthesis.
